Can the S&P 500 reliably beat a tenge deposit?

A Kazakh saver can lock in double-digit bank rates at home or buy America’s 500 largest companies. We follow 320 months of official data to see which choice actually built more wealth, after taxes, for every possible starting date.

Abstract

Kazakhstan offers savers unusually high nominal deposit rates, averaging 10.8% on new tenge term deposits since 1999, while its currency lost 70% of its value against the US dollar. We ask whether an investment in the S&P 500, converted into tenge and taxed as a Kazakh resident, consistently outperforms a tenge deposit. Using official monthly exchange rates and deposit rates from the National Bank of Kazakhstan and the S&P 500 total-return index from December 1999 to August 2026, we evaluate every possible entry month across horizons of 1 to 20 years, map the full entry-and-exit space, decompose returns into market and currency components, and stress-test the results with a block bootstrap.

Over the full period the S&P 500 returned 12.5% a year after tax against 11.3% for the deposit. The edge is neither large nor reliable: the index won in only 52–60% of windows up to five years, rising to 90–93% at 15–20 years, and to 67% under bootstrap resampling. The advantage came almost entirely from three tenge devaluations (2009, 2014, 2015); excluding those years, the deposit led by 2.8 percentage points a year. At today’s 14.7% deposit rate, the tenge would need to weaken by about 7.5% a year for equities merely to break even.

Keywords: household portfolio choice, S&P 500, bank deposits, tenge, currency risk, emerging markets  ·  JEL: G11, E43, F31

Introduction

For most families in Kazakhstan, “saving” means a bank deposit. It is simple, insured by the Kazakhstan Deposit Insurance Fund, exempt from personal income tax, and in recent years has paid more than 14% a year. A growing number of retail brokers now also offer direct access to US exchange-traded funds that track the S&P 500.

The comparison is less obvious than it looks. In dollars, the S&P 500 has been one of the best-performing asset classes of the past century. But a Kazakh saver does not spend dollars. The relevant question is what the index earns in tenge, net of foreign taxes and conversion costs, compared with a local deposit whose rate already compensates for inflation and expected depreciation.

This paper tests four hypotheses, stated before running the analysis. The verdicts are summarised below and supported in Sections 4 and 5.

Why “consistently” matters. A strategy that wins on average but loses for a decade after an unlucky start date is a poor fit for a family saving toward tuition or a home. We therefore measure the share of starting dates on which equities won, not just the average.

Data

All series are monthly, from December 1999 (the first month for which the National Bank’s archive returns an official month-end rate) to August 2026. Deposit rates are the National Bank’s weighted-average rates on new term deposits of individuals, reported separately for tenge and foreign currency. Equity returns use the S&P 500 Total Return index, which reinvests dividends.

As a data-quality check, the ETF SPY tracks the index to within 0.08% a year over the sample, which is almost exactly its expense ratio.

A currency regime change. Until August 2015 the tenge was managed against the dollar and moved in discrete steps. Since then it has floated, so depreciation arrives gradually rather than in single overnight jumps.

Methodology

We compare five strategies from the perspective of a Kazakh tax resident who measures wealth in tenge: a tenge term deposit, a US-dollar deposit, the S&P 500 before and after costs, and a 50/50 mix of the deposit and the index. Monthly returns are compounded; the deposit rate observed in month t−1 is earned in month t.

i is the tenge deposit rate; RTR the index total return in USD; D its dividend component, taxed 15% at source under the US–Kazakhstan treaty (form W-8BEN); 0.1% a year covers fund and broker fees; Δe is the change in USD/KZT. On exit (3), 0.5% covers conversion spreads and a 10% personal income tax applies to the tenge gain on foreign securities not traded on KASE or AIX. Interest on deposits in Kazakh banks is tax-exempt. xh is the annualised excess return over a horizon of h years.

Three designs address different questions. Rolling windows evaluate every entry month for horizons of 1–20 years (309 one-year windows down to 81 twenty-year windows). An entry–exit matrix covers every pair of calendar years. A moving-block bootstrap (Künsch, 1989) draws 10,000 synthetic paths from 24-month blocks of joint history. It keeps the co-movement of equities, the exchange rate and deposit rates while breaking the one historical order of events.

4

Results

4.1The full period: a narrow win

One million tenge placed in a deposit in December 1999 grew to 17.5 million by August 2026. The same sum in the S&P 500 would have been worth 23.3 million after every tax and fee: 12.5% a year against 11.3%. Adjusted for Kazakh inflation of 8.75% a year, that is 3.5% against 2.4% in real terms.

The path matters as much as the destination. Measured by what they could actually withdraw, the equity investor was behind the depositor for twenty years, from January 2000 until mid-2020. At the trough in January 2009 they held just 22% of the depositor’s balance.

4.2Year by year: frequent small losses, rare large wins

The index beat the deposit in 14 of 26 calendar years, and the distribution is lopsided. The deposit’s wins cluster in equity bear markets (2000–2002, 2008, 2022). The index’s largest wins, +41 points in 2009 and +74 points in 2015, coincide with devaluations.

4.3Consistency depends on the horizon

This is the central test. For horizons of one to five years, the probability that the S&P 500 beat the deposit was barely better than a coin flip: 52% to 60% of starting months. Only at 15 years and beyond did equities win in nine windows out of ten.

Worst ten-year start: September 2000. An investor who bought then trailed the deposit by 11.9 percentage points a year for a decade.

4.5

The currency channel

In dollars, the S&P 500 returned 8.4% a year, less than the 11.3% earned on the tenge deposit. Every point of the equity advantage in tenge came from the exchange rate.

This pattern echoes what economists call the forward-premium puzzle (Fama, 1984): high-rate currencies tend not to depreciate as much as their interest differential implies. Kazakh banks paid on average 6.6 percentage points more on tenge than on foreign-currency deposits, and in 20 of 26 years that premium exceeded the tenge’s actual depreciation, so holding tenge paid.

The other six years were violent: +23% in 2009, +19% in 2014 and +86% in 2015. Remove 2009, 2014 and 2015 from the sample and the deposit beats the S&P 500 by about 2.8 percentage points a year.

A natural hedge. Monthly S&P 500 returns and changes in USD/KZT are negatively correlated (−0.23). When global markets fall, the tenge tends to weaken, which cushions the loss for a tenge-based investor.

4.6The price of the premium is risk

The deposit never recorded a negative month in 320 months, and its worst twelve months still returned +7.5%. The S&P 500 in tenge fell 48% from peak to trough, and its worst twelve months lost 39%. Each unit of volatility bought only about 0.07 units of excess return. A simple 50/50 mix captured most of the equity premium, at 12.0% a year, with a maximum drawdown of just 8%.

4.7Saving every month changes the picture

Real households save from income rather than investing a lump sum. With a contribution of 100,000 ₸ at the start of every month, timing risk shrinks sharply: contributions made after the 2008 crash bought cheaply and made up for an unlucky start. Over 15-year saving periods, the S&P 500 finished ahead in all 141 windows.

5

Robustness and the view from today

Twenty-six years contain only two or three independent decades, so overlapping windows overstate how much we know. The block bootstrap removes the luck of the order in which crashes and devaluations happened to arrive. The result is more sober: the probability of beating the deposit rises only from 53% at one year to 67% at twenty years, against 93% in the historical windows.

Why the gap? History delivered its worst equity decade (2000–2009) before its largest devaluations, and later windows benefited from both recoveries at once. Resampling mixes these episodes in random order, which widens the range of outcomes.

Starting conditions today are also unusual. The weighted-average rate on new tenge term deposits is 14.7%, well above its 10.8% historical mean. Table 7 shows the annual tenge depreciation needed for the S&P 500 simply to match the deposit.

Conclusion

The S&P 500 beat the tenge deposit, but not reliably. It won over the long run, and mostly because the tenge fell.

Over the full period: yes, narrowly

12.5% versus 11.3% a year after tax, or 23.3 million ₸ versus 17.5 million from one million.

Up to five years: a coin flip

The index led in only 52–60% of windows. For short-term goals, the deposit was the more dependable choice.

Fifteen years and longer: usually

90–93% of historical windows, and 100% for monthly savers over 15 years. Bootstrap resampling puts the odds closer to two in three.

The currency did the work

In dollars the index earned less than the deposit rate. Devaluations in 2009, 2014 and 2015 created the entire advantage.

Today’s bar is high

With a 14.7% deposit rate and 8% dollar returns, the tenge must weaken by about 7.5% a year for equities just to break even.

“And”, not “or”

A deposit suits emergency funds and near-term goals; global equities suit goals 15 or more years away and diversify currency risk. A 50/50 mix earned 12.0% with an 8% drawdown.

8

Limitations

  • Average rates are not offered rates. Individual banks may pay more or less than the weighted average, and savings deposits restrict early withdrawal.
  • Overlapping windows are not independent. Eighty-one twenty-year windows are far from eighty-one separate experiments.
  • Survivorship bias. The United States was the most successful equity market of the past century (Dimson, Marsh & Staunton, 2002), so choosing the S&P 500 with hindsight flatters equities.
  • Simplified taxation. A new Tax Code took effect in Kazakhstan on 1 January 2026. Actual liabilities depend on the broker, the exchange and total income.
  • Behaviour is ignored. Selling during a 48% drawdown turns a paper loss into a permanent one.
  • Deposit insurance. Deposits are guaranteed by KDIF up to statutory limits; equities carry no such protection.
